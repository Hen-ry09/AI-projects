# Ghanaian Traditions & Customs

## Naming Ceremonies (Outdooring)
- Ghanaian babies are formally named on the 7th or 8th day after birth (sometimes later)
- The child is brought outside for the first time (hence "outdooring")
- The father whispers the child's name into their ear three times
- Water, wine, and gin are offered to ancestors and poured on the ground
- **Traveler tip**: If invited, bring a gift (baby clothes, diapers, money). Dress formally

## Chieftaincy
- Traditional leaders (chiefs/queen mothers) are custodians of land and culture
- They are addressed as "Nana" — never by their first name
- Chieftaincy disputes are serious — avoid getting involved or commenting
- The Golden Stool (Ashanti) and other stools symbolize the soul of the nation

## Marriage Customs
- Traditional marriage ("engagement") is a elaborate ceremony separate from the church/wedding
- The groom's family presents a dowry (drinks, cloth, jewelry, money)
- The bride's family often "refuses" the first request — negotiation is part of the ritual
- **Traveler tip**: If invited, wear traditional attire, bring a gift, and expect a long (but joyful) ceremony

## Funeral Traditions
- Funerals in Ghana are major social events — often more elaborate than weddings
- They typically happen on weekends (Friday–Sunday) to allow attendance
- Mourners wear black and red (older person) or black and white (young person)
- The deceased is often buried in a fantasy coffin shaped like their profession or passion
- **Traveler tip**: Never attend a funeral uninvited. If invited, wear black/white respectfully

## Religious Practices
- Ghana is deeply religious: ~71% Christian, ~20% Muslim, ~9% traditional/other
- Sunday church services are widely observed — expect traffic disruptions near churches
- Friday prayers are observed in Muslim communities
- Traditional priests and shrines are still active in rural areas

## Food Traditions
- Meals are often shared from a communal bowl (family style)
- Fufu and light soup is eaten with the right hand — form a ball of fufu, dip, and swallow without chewing
- It's polite to say "Thank you" ("Medaase" in Twi) after a meal
- Never refuse food from an elder — it's a serious offense
